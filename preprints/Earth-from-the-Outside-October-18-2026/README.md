## Abstract

We reverse the usual exoplanet observing problem: given Earth and the Sun, which external locations could discover the planet, assess its habitability, measure evidence of a biosphere, or detect technology, and on what timescales? We combine a frozen 2,034-source Gaia-based Earth Transit Zone catalog, a published Virtual Planetary Laboratory quadrature spectrum, photon-counting calculations, 300,000 random viewing configurations, and explicit light-cone constraints. Within the supplied catalog union, a fixed-plane, zero-radial-velocity calculation yields 1,404 transit-zone members at catalog-coordinate epoch 2026.8; a separate first-order apparent-to-kinematic timing sensitivity yields 1,400. Earth produces an 83.9 ppm central transit lasting 13.1 h, whereas a 10 km atmospheric annulus contributes only 0.263 ppm. Under specified 6 m and 15 m space-coronagraph scenarios, a local oxygen A-band index in the reference spectrum attains expected signal-to-noise ratio five in 94.3 h and 6.2 h, respectively, at 10 pc. Complete-index angular access ends at 12.27 and 30.67 pc for the adopted 3λ/D inner working angle. These are conditional feature-statistic sensitivities, not atmospheric retrievals or life probabilities. A historical 20 TW narrowband radar scenario has a sensitivity-only range of 660 light-years for the chosen receiver, but our rounded 1997.0-2020.0 emission-era model occupies a 6.8-29.8 light-year shell at epoch 2026.8, with sparse beam illumination. Nearby observers sample broadly modern oxygenated Earth but different technological epochs. The evidential stages need not occur in order: an intercepted artificial transmission can precede optical planet characterization. The calculations support a reproducible, assumption-dependent atlas of observing opportunities; they do not identify extraterrestrial civilizations.
