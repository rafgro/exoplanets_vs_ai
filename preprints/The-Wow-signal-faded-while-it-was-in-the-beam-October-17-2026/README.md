## Abstract

The 1977 “Wow!” signal survives as six quantized signal-to-noise values (6EQUJ5) from one 10 kHz channel of the Ohio State Big Ear telescope. We re-derive what those six numbers constrain with a forward model of the instrument that includes, for the first time, the recorder algorithm (N50CH running baseline and σ, floor quantization, 10 s integration in 12 s records) and an interval-censored likelihood. Three results are new. (i) The profile is asymmetric: the leading wing is 1.7 units brighter and the trailing wing 1.5 units fainter than any symmetric beam allows; a symmetric crossing reproduces data this skewed in 0.37% of Monte-Carlo trials (≈ 2.9σ), while a profile that fades with an e-folding time of 17+4 −3 s starting ≈ 7 s after beam centre fits essentially perfectly. The fade explains the blank second beam 172 s later without additional assumptions and places the detection in the first beam to sweep the source with probability 0.89 (Bayes factor 8.4), i.e. at RA 19h27m55srather than the commonly quoted 19h25m02s. (ii) The second local-oscillator reading at the peak, under the Galactic-standard-of-rest tracking described by the observatory’s staff, implies a frequency reference of 120.00 MHz rather than the 120.1 MHz adopted in the 2025 reanalysis, moving the line from 1420.726 to 1420.826 MHz and VLSR from −74 to −95 km s−1(the 2025 paper itself gives both reference values, and its calibrating clouds post-date the relevant LO change); all candidate velocities are forbidden for circular rotation at l = 11.6 ◦ and fall in the velocity range of the compact-clump HVC complex GCN. (iii) The 1977 August 15 continuum chart, digitised here, gives the beam on the night: HPBW 36±1.5 s on both horns and a beam separation of 181 ± 4 s rather than the assumed 172.4 s; against that beam the Wow! core is unresolved and the anomaly reduces to a leading excess of ≈ 10% of peak about 30 s before beam centre, which neither horn’s measured response reproduces. Recorder absorption of the first record raises the flux lower limit to ≳ 300 Jy. Simulations exclude every steady source, including scintillating ones; a stochastic repeater sits at 1.0% likelihood after 193.5 h of follow-up. A natural origin requires coherent 21-cm emission from a cold, dense, tens-of-AU knot pumped by a flare whose geometry forces a circumstellar host and a flare that reached Earth within a minute of the pulse; an artificial origin is technologically undemanding but requires a 10−5–10−2timing coincidence. We estimate posterior odds of roughly 50–60% anthropogenic or instrumental, 25–35% natural transient and ≲ 10% artificial extraterrestrial, and identify four archival tests costing hours to days that would reorder them.
