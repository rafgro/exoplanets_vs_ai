## Abstract

Outer giants coexist with inner small planets when formation supplies the inner system and subsequent perturbations avoid destructive differential orbital excitation. We examine the dynamical part of this statement with a public-catalog audit, a screen of 1,719 synthetic architectures, 694 direct integration jobs including controls and numerical checks, and 1,008 reduced secular histories. In 24 deliberately stress-selected architectures, coherent eccentricity vectors yield 39/48 encounter-free realizations over 20,000 yr, compared with 11/48 circular starts; halving the timestep gives 39/48 and 15/48. Removing the giant yields 48/48, whereas shuffling inner apses at fixed eccentricity magnitudes yields 1/48. Selected coherent realizations persist for one million years, but others fail. Prescribed slow giant excitation and gradual removal of inner damping can generate low free eccentricity from circular starts. These histories are reduced disk models, and an apparent faster-growth survival disadvantage disappears on numerical refinement. Scattering produces both weakly forced survivors and damaging trajectories. A fixed California Legacy Survey selection contains four coexistence hosts among 58 detected-giant hosts. Their modeled coherent eccentricity harmonics are only 10−4–2 × 10−2 m s−1, making this sample a weak test of orbital coherence. Coexistence is therefore favored by weak differential forcing, sufficiently strong internal coupling or additional precession, and compatible eccentricity vectors populated by favorable histories. The calculations support this conditional explanation, but do not establish a universal giant-mass, eccentricity, or separation threshold, a population survival fraction, or a unique formation pathway.
